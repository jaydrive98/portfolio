Data Engineering · Google Cloud

EventsAir → BigQuery → HubSpot
Real-Time Pipeline

A production, event-driven pipeline on Google Cloud that takes live registration data from our events platform, lands it in a BigQuery warehouse, and keeps the CRM in sync — automatically, within seconds, and without anyone touching a spreadsheet.

The problem

Registration data that lived everywhere and nowhere

Every summit, conference and expo generates a flood of registrations in EventsAir, our events platform. That data is where the money is — who's coming, what they paid, which sessions and functions they signed up for, which exhibitors booked stands. But it was trapped inside the platform. Getting it to the sales, marketing and leadership teams meant manual exports, copy-paste, and spreadsheets full of personal data floating around inboxes.

The brief was simple to say and hard to do: make that data reliable, live, and in the tools people already use — a proper BigQuery warehouse for analytics and dashboards, and the HubSpot CRM for sales and marketing — with no manual step in the middle, and full POPIA/GDPR discipline over the personal data moving through it.

How it works

The pipeline, end to end

EventsAirWebhook fires on any change
→
ReceiverVerifies signature, audits, queues
→
ProcessorFetches, cleans & transforms
→
BigQueryWarehouse: live + history
→
HubSpotCRM synced in real time

The moment anything changes in EventsAir, a webhook fires. A public receiver service checks the message is genuinely from EventsAir (HMAC signature), writes a raw audit record, and drops a job onto a queue. A private processor service picks the job up, pulls the full, up-to-date contact from the EventsAir GraphQL API, transforms it, and writes it to BigQuery — both a current-state table and an append-only history. On success it hands off to the HubSpot sync, and a separate branch pushes speakers into ClickUp to trigger downstream work.

Each stage is its own service with its own retries and timeout, connected by queues rather than direct calls. That decoupling is the whole point: a HubSpot rate-limit can't take down the BigQuery write, and any single step can fail and retry on its own without losing the event.

Engineering highlights

The interesting bits

Decoupled by design

Receiver, processor, HubSpot sync, speaker automation and the CDC sweeper are separate Cloud Run services joined by Cloud Tasks queues — each with its own retries, so a slow downstream never blocks an upstream write.

Idempotent writes

BigQuery writes use a staging-table MERGE keyed on natural IDs, and history tables dedupe on a content hash — so a webhook delivered twice never creates duplicate or conflicting rows.

Self-healing event discovery

A registration for an unknown event doesn't crash the pipeline — it triggers automatic discovery that infers the brand and portfolio, provisions the event in both BigQuery and HubSpot, and carries on.

Fuzzy taxonomy matching

Free-text industry and audience answers are normalised against a controlled taxonomy in three tiers — exact, punctuation-insensitive, then Levenshtein fuzzy match — with a safety kill-switch and alert when nothing fits.

CDC catch-up sweeper

A scheduled change-data-capture sweeper finds recently modified contacts and replays them through the same processor path, so nothing is lost if a webhook is ever missed.

Point-in-time snapshots

A weekly job captures registration and function snapshots into BigQuery, powering week-over-week trend reporting that the live tables alone can't give you.

Security & trust boundaries

Only the receiver is public, gated by EventsAir signature checks. Every internal call authenticates with Google-signed OIDC tokens, each service runs as its own identity, and secrets stay in Secret Manager.

Cost-aware & observable

Temporary staging tables auto-expire, DML retries back off under BigQuery concurrency limits, and structured logs with trace IDs plus a dead-letter queue make failures visible and replayable.

The evolution

From one big function to a resilient system

The first version that proved the concept in production was a single Cloud Function doing everything at once — receive, fetch, transform, write to BigQuery, sync HubSpot. It worked, but one big function meant one shared timeout, no clean way to retry a single step, and history rows that could duplicate on a retry.

I re-architected it into the decoupled, multi-service design above — splitting the responsibilities, introducing queues and dead-letter handling, making writes idempotent, and hardening the security boundaries. Same job, far more resilient, and finally testable service by service.
